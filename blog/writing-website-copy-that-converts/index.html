    Writing website copy that converts

    October 5, 2026 · Desanya Studio

    A beautiful website with vague words is a nice-looking brochure. A plain website with clear words is a sales tool. The best sites have both, but if you have to choose, choose clarity.

    "Converts" just means the visitor does what you hoped they'd do: calls, books, buys, fills out the form, or gets directions. Copy is how you get them there.

    Start with one question: who is this for?

    Before you write a word, picture one real customer. Not "everyone in Tennessee," but one person with one problem. A homeowner in Knoxville whose AC quit in July. A family in Chattanooga looking for a dog groomer they can trust. A small business owner who needs a website but doesn't know where to start.

    Write to that person. Use their words, not your industry's. Customers rarely search for "comprehensive residential climate solutions." They search for "AC repair near me."

    The headline: say what you do, plainly

    Your homepage headline has one job: tell visitors they're in the right place. Within a few seconds they should know:

    1. What you do
    2. Who you do it for
    3. Where (if you're local)

    "Quality you can trust" could belong to anyone. "Family-owned plumbing for Maryville and Knoxville homes" belongs to you.

    A short line under the headline can add the "why you": fast response, upfront pricing, a guarantee, years in the community. Keep it to something you can back up.

    Write benefits, then back them with features

    A feature is what something is. A benefit is what it does for the customer.

    • Feature: "Mobile-first design." Benefit: "Customers can find your number and call you from their phone in one tap."
    • Feature: "Monthly care plan." Benefit: "Your site stays current without you thinking about it."

    Lead with the benefit. Follow with the feature as proof.

    Make every page earn its next step

    Every page should end with one clear action, and the button should say exactly what happens next. "Submit" and "Learn more" are vague. "Book intro," "Call for a free estimate," and "See this week's puppies" tell people what they're getting.

    One primary action per page is usually enough. If you give visitors five buttons, they often pick none.

    Answer the questions people are afraid to ask

    The biggest reasons people don't contact a business are uncertainty and friction. What does it cost? How long does it take? Do you serve my area? What happens after I call?

    Answer those on the page. Publish prices if you can. Desanya Studio lists every package openly, from $150 website copywriting to a $2,500 website, because a visitor who already knows the price is a visitor who's ready to talk. If you can't publish exact prices, give a starting point or explain how quotes work.

    Use proof, and keep it honest

    Real proof beats big claims. Real photos of your work. Short, genuine quotes from customers (with their permission). Specific results you can verify.

    For example: Sherman's Shih Tzu Puppies, a family breeder in Niota, East Tennessee, recorded 2,186 Google Business Profile interactions (calls, messages, directions and website clicks) from May through October 2026 with a real website and a managed Google profile working together. That's a number you can stand behind, which is the only kind worth putting on a website.

    Never invent numbers, ratings, or reviews. Customers notice, and trust is hard to win back.

    Cut, then cut again

    Most first drafts are twice as long as they need to be. Read every sentence and ask: does a customer need this to make a decision? If not, cut it.

    Short paragraphs. Clear subheadings. Bullet points for lists. People skim first and read second, so make the skim count.

    Read it out loud

    The easiest editing trick there is: read your copy aloud. If you stumble, rewrite it. If it sounds like a brochure, make it sound like you. If you wouldn't say it to a customer across the counter, don't put it on your website.
